# The rewrite-search formalism

## What this record decides

[The optimizer contract](../../compiler/optimizer.md#bounded-hierarchical-search) says that "a Cascades-style memo is one possible implementation technique, not a committed architecture", and deliberately commits to the search's *obligations* rather than its mechanism. This record closes that gap against the primary literature. Its conclusion, stated first so a reader can go looking for the refutation rather than the argument:

**The selected formalism is a staged combination in which every stage retains legal alternatives without committing, and no stage prunes a semantic alternative on estimated cost. Its concrete shape is the one Tiler is already building — baseline-preserving semantic exploration feeding an independently-verified physical memo — and the durable commitment this record adds is the *prohibition* on the two shapes that are cheaper and lose: destructive cost-pruned rewriting, and equality saturation as the whole search.** Equality saturation is not eliminated as a component. It is eliminated as the search, and the one place it would be worth adopting is named below with an activation trigger.

Every source cited here is preserved or fingerprinted under [`sources/`](sources/README.md); every claim about Tiler's own code was read in the tree at commit `38ccda41` and states the command that reproduces it.

## Part 0 — what Tiler's search actually is today

A tractability claim about a formalism is a claim about a vocabulary, and importing a paper's benchmark vocabulary would make the claim about the wrong system. This section states Tiler's, measured against source rather than recalled.

**Fact — the registered rewrite vocabulary is three rules, of which two are algebraic and both are one rule schema instantiated at two operations.** `crates/tiler-compiler/src/rewrite.rs` declares `tiler.normalize/common-subexpression.v1@1`, `tiler.algebraic/ordered-reassociate-add-f32.v1@1`, and `tiler.algebraic/ordered-reassociate-multiply-f32.v1@1`. A fourth `RewriteRuleIdentity::new` call, `tiler.pipeline/canonical-semantic-baseline.v1@1` in `pipeline.rs`, labels the unmodified baseline so it can travel through the same proposal channel; it rewrites nothing. Reproduce with `grep -rn 'RewriteRuleIdentity::new' crates/ --include='*.rs'`, which returns four non-test lines, and `grep -rn 'impl RewriteRuleProvider' crates/ --include='*.rs'`, which returns ten of which eight are inside `mod tests`.

**Fact — the vocabulary cannot grow without a source edit, which bounds it structurally rather than by count.** `AlgebraicRuleConfiguration` in `crates/tiler-compiler/src/normalize.rs` is a struct with exactly two `bool` fields, `add` and `multiply`; there is no registry, map, or vector of algebraic rules, and the stage-3 driver iterates a literal two-element array. Every enabled rule contributes at most one whole-program proposal, so the stage-3 candidate set is at most three programs — baseline, add-reassociated, multiply-reassociated — *regardless of program size*.

**Fact — the IR declares exactly one algebraic law.** `OperationAlgebraicCapabilities` (`crates/tiler-ir/src/semantic/operation.rs`) has a single declarable field, `ordered_associativity`, granted to exactly one capability set (`arithmetic_f32_algebraic_capabilities()` in `registry.rs`), which two of the eighteen registered operation families hold. Eighteen is the count of `pub fn *_op() -> OpKey` declarations under `crates/tiler-ir/src/semantic/`.

**Fact — a memo already exists, and it is not a Cascades memo.** `crates/tiler-compiler/src/cover.rs` carries `MemoEntry`, a `CoverageMask` key, and a `Partitioner` holding `memo: BTreeMap<CoverageMask, MemoEntry>`, with an in-source soundness argument that admissibility depends on the coverage state alone. That is a memo over *graph partitions keyed by a per-operation coverage vector*. It has no group/expression separation, no equivalence classes, and holds no logical alternatives. The optimizer contract [reserves the word `memo`](../../compiler/optimizer.md#bounded-hierarchical-search) for a structure that groups equivalence classes and performs goal-directed property search, and this one is correctly not called that.

**Fact — there is no e-graph anywhere, and no cost authority for pruning semantic alternatives.** `grep -rni "egraph\|e-graph\|equality.saturation" crates/ --include='*.rs'` returns five hits, all false positives on `ForeignCoverageGraph` and "whole-graph"; no `egg` or `egglog` dependency appears in any manifest or in `Cargo.lock`. The cost types that exist (`PhysicalCostEstimate`, `PlanStructuralCost`, `CoverCost`, `ComponentCost`) are all `pub(crate)` in `tiler-compiler` and all physical; [the cost-model contract](../../compiler/cost-model.md) is `spike-only`.

**Inference.** At this vocabulary every formalism in the literature is tractable, including brute force, so *no* choice here can be justified on today's measured search cost. The choice has to be justified on the obligations the contract already carries and on what the vocabulary becomes when the capability set the [flash-class attention ticket](../../../tickets/derive-the-capability-set-for-search-discovered-flash-class-attention-kernels.md) derives actually lands. That is the frame for everything below, and it is also why this record proposes a tractability experiment rather than running one: there is nothing yet to measure.

## Part 1 — the database optimizer lineage

**Relevance question.** This repository's architecture is explicitly "DataFusion for tensor compute". What does the database memo transfer, and what does it not, when candidates carry physical feasibility predicates and numerical permissions rather than only cost?

**Selinger et al. 1979 (`selinger-sigmod-1979`)** contributes two mechanisms. Bottom-up dynamic programming over join orders is the ancestor of every memo below. *Interesting orders* is the more important one and the one Tiler already has: a plan that is not locally cheapest is retained because a property it guarantees may pay for itself upstream, so the search keeps one best plan **per interesting property** rather than one best plan. Tiler's [boundary requirements and guarantees](../../compiler/optimizer.md#boundary-requirements-and-guarantees) — canonical keys, satisfaction and subsumption, child-requirement derivation, dominance, and the explicit statement that "interesting boundary properties such as useful unit-stride axes are retained on a bounded Pareto frontier even when they are not locally cheapest" — is interesting orders generalized to a property lattice. That transfer is complete and already made.

**Volcano (`volcano-icde-1993`)** contributes the search shape: `FindBestPlan(LogExpr, PhysProp, Limit)`, memoized on the pair of a logical expression and a physical property vector, driven top-down and goal-directed — the paper's own term is "directed dynamic programming", contrasted with EXODUS's forward-chaining as backward chaining. Four of its structures map onto Tiler's exactly, and one does not.

- The **physical property vector** is an abstract data type whose only required operation is a *cover* comparison — does a synthesized property satisfy a required one. Tiler's boundary-property satisfaction and subsumption relation is the same thing with more dimensions and an explicit admission rule for adding one.
- **Enforcers** are physical operators with no logical counterpart, inserted to supply a property. Tiler's enforcer list (contiguous materialization, layout conversion, encoding repacking) is the same concept, and the optimizer contract's argument that a dtype cast is *not* an enforcer is a sharper version of Volcano's own implicit rule that an enforcer changes representation and not value.
- The **excluding physical property vector** is the subtlest transfer and Tiler has not yet needed it: when Volcano optimizes a sort's input it forbids algorithms that already deliver sort order, so merge-join is not considered under a sort. The general principle is that inserting an enforcer must exclude the property being enforced from its own input's search, or the search re-derives the property it is about to pay for. Tiler's enforcer insertion is "cycle-checked" per the contract; a cycle check is a weaker statement than an exclusion vector and the difference is a real gap, filed below. **Closed 2026-08-06 by [the enforcer input-property exclusion record](enforcer-input-property-exclusion.md), which read the paper's own passage and the planner at source and found the redundancy unreachable — not merely because enforcers are unimplemented, but because the excluding vector is the second property parameter of a goal-directed search whose first parameter Tiler does not construct.** That record also corrects one mapping this bullet leans on: Tiler's requirement/guarantee pair is Volcano's *derived input* property vector, not its `PhysProp` goal, so "a third vector beside requirement and guarantee" was never the right shape for the answer.
- **Branch-and-bound cost limits** passed down into subexpression optimization are Volcano's pruning mechanism. Tiler deliberately does not have this at the semantic level, and Part 5 explains why that absence is load-bearing rather than missing.

**Cascades (`cascades-debull-18-3-1995`)** is Volcano's successor and contributes the parts most often quoted at Tiler. Its memo separates *groups* (equivalence classes of logically equivalent expressions) from *expressions* within a group. Its optimization tasks are reorderable objects rather than procedure calls, so the search order is data. Rules carry **promise** functions (a real-valued estimate of how useful a rule might be, consulted before exploration) and **condition** functions (a boolean applicability check, consulted after). A **pattern memory** per group records which patterns a group has been explored for, so the same group is not re-expanded for the same pattern. Enforcers become ordinary rules rather than special-cased operators. Each expression carries a bitmap of rules already applied to it.

Two Cascades sentences are the ones this record's elimination turns on, and both are the paper being honest about its own limits. On exploration: "If there is no guidance indicating which rule might lead to expressions matching the given pattern, exhaustive enumeration of all equivalent logical expressions cannot be avoided." And on the price of that guidance: "if such guidance is incorrect, incorrect pruning of the search space may occur and the Cascades optimizer's effectiveness might suffer. Thus, it is very important that such guidance be correct."

**Orca (`orca-sigmod-2014`)** is Cascades in production — "a modern top-down query optimizer based on the Cascades optimization framework" — and it is cited here because an elimination that ignored the strongest deployed instance of a candidate would not be an elimination. Three of its mechanisms bear on Tiler. Its Memo is Cascades' groups and group expressions unchanged. Its **property-enforcement framework** is genuinely extensible and driven by "formal property specifications", with required properties covering sort order, distribution, "output columns, rewindability, common table expressions and data partitioning", and with each operator controlling enforcer placement — the closest thing in the DB literature to Tiler's admission rule for a new boundary-property dimension. And its **multi-stage optimization** defines a stage as "a complete optimization workflow using a subset of transformation rules and (optional) time-out and cost threshold", terminating when a plan beats the threshold, the time-out fires, or the rule subset is exhausted. That is the DB lineage's answer to budgets, and Part 6 states exactly where it stops short of Tiler's.

**Inference — what the DB memo does not transfer, and it is not cost.** The memo transfers cleanly. What does not transfer is the assumption underneath its pruning: in a relational optimizer, *every* expression in a group is a legal plan, and the only question is which is cheapest, so pruning on cost is total. In Tiler an alternative can be **infeasible** — the target does not realize it — or **numerically impermissible** — the caller's contract does not grant the permission the rewrite consumes — and both are hard answers that a cost comparison must never express. The optimizer contract already enforces this by absent conversions rather than by rule: the feasibility authority has no cost input, and `estimate.rs` offers no conversion from an uncertain estimate into anything feasibility consults. The consequence for formalism choice is that Tiler's search has *three* pruning authorities where Cascades has one, they are ordered (numerical contract preference, then feasibility, then cost), and only the last is an estimate. A formalism whose only retention mechanism is a cost comparison cannot express that ordering.

## Part 2 — equality saturation

**Relevance question.** Is e-graph saturation over Tiler's declared rewrites tractable at the vocabulary's size, how does extraction interact with a cost authority that does not exist yet, and what does saturation do to explain output and deterministic budgets?

**Tate et al. (`eqsat-lmcs-7-1-10-2011`)** states the motivating problem exactly as the ticket does: destructive rewriting suffers phase ordering, and "profitability heuristics, which decide whether or not to apply a given optimization, tend to" be local. Its answer is to make the IR non-destructive — a Program Expression Graph whose nodes are merged into equivalence classes, an E-PEG — so that a **global profitability heuristic** can pick the final program after all equalities have been discovered. In the prototype that heuristic is a Pseudo-Boolean solver over a static per-node cost model. The lineage of "the extractor is an ILP-shaped solver" starts here.

**Denali (`denali-pldi-2002`) is where this record's oldest evidence lives, and it was acquired on 2026-08-05 expecting to say one thing and found to say another.** Part 9 and the source record both described Denali as goal-directed *rather than* saturating. That is wrong, and the correction is more useful than the claim it replaces. Denali's matching phase instantiates axioms into the E-graph "until a quiescent state is reached in which the E-graph records all relevant instances of axioms" — saturation — and the goal-direction lives entirely in the *selection* phase, where a cycle-bound conjecture ("No program of the target architecture computes the values of the goal terms within K cycles") is refuted by a SAT solver. Three facts it does supply, all read, and each one bears on a different part of this record. **(1)** It is the earliest statement in this corpus that a practical e-graph run is budget-terminated rather than saturated, *and* that the budget is what costs the answer its optimality: the paper calls its output "near-optimal" instead of "optimal", and the first reason it gives is "if the heuristics that are designed to keep the matcher from running forever don't mistakenly stop it from running long enough". That is `egg`'s `is_saturated_or_timeout` and its consequence, nineteen years earlier. **(2)** Saturation, not extraction, is where the time goes: the four-byte-swap problem takes "just over a minute" of which "less than 0.3 seconds is spent in the SAT solver", and a checksum loop body of "10 cycles and 31 instructions" took "about 4 hours". **(3)** The paper states the representation's trade in one sentence and then states its price in the next — "instead of rewriting A as B, it records A = B in its data structure, leaving both A and B around", against "Matching in an E-graph is more expensive than matching a pattern against a simple term DAG. Also, many matches are required to reach quiescence, and the quiescent state may be quite a large E-graph." Its measurements are 2002 hardware and a preliminary prototype aimed at "inner loops and critical subroutines"; what transfers is the ratio between the phases and the disclaimer's stated reason, not the wall-clock numbers.

**egg (`egg-arxiv-2004.03082v3`)** is the engineering that made this practical, and it contributes the fact that matters most for a system with deterministic budgets. Its own pseudocode for the workflow is `while not egraph.is_saturated_or_timeout()`, and its prose is explicit that the rules run "until the e-graph is saturated (or a timeout is reached)". *Rebuilding* — deferring congruence-invariant restoration to phase boundaries — is what makes the workload fast, and *e-class analyses* are the extension point for domain facts. Neither changes the budget contract: a practical equality-saturation run is a budgeted run, and its termination condition is a resource limit far more often than a fixed point.

**egglog (`egglog-arxiv-2304.04332v4`)** is "a fixpoint reasoning system that unifies Datalog and equality saturation", and its own summary of what the Datalog side contributes is "efficient incremental execution, cooperating analyses, and lattice-based reasoning". Its section 4.3 is *semi-naïve evaluation*: each iteration maintains a differential so the engine stops "discovering same facts over and over again". This is the one development in the corpus that would change the arithmetic of a Tiler stage-1 e-graph, because it turns repeated whole-e-graph matching into incremental matching over newly derived facts — and "cooperating analyses" is the mechanism by which an operation-owned algebraic capability could gate a rule from inside the engine rather than outside it.

**Tensat (`tensat-arxiv-2101.01332v2`)** is the only published attempt to run equality saturation over a tensor computation graph at production vocabulary size, and it reports both a win and two structural costs. The win: graphs "up to 16% faster" than TASO's backtracking search while "reducing the optimization time by up to 300x". The costs are the parts Tiler must read as warnings rather than results.

- **A valid rewrite can introduce a cycle into the e-graph.** Tensat states that the e-graph "can (and likely will) contain cycles" while an extracted graph must not, and handles it either with an ILP acyclicity constraint or by filtering cycles during exploration. Tiler's semantic IR is a DAG and its region legality rests on *convexity* — "a path between two included operations may not leave and re-enter the region" — which is a DAG-level property. A representation that routinely goes cyclic and repairs it at extraction time is putting a structural invariant Tiler currently maintains by construction into a post-hoc constraint.
- **The acyclicity constraint is what makes extraction the bottleneck.** Tensat reports the ILP solver struggling to find a feasible solution because of it, which is why the cycle filtering exists at all.

**TASO (`taso-sosp-2019`)** is adjacent and answers a different question: where a rewrite's soundness obligation lives. TASO *generates* candidate substitutions from operator building blocks and then discharges each against declared operator properties using a first-order theorem prover, which is how it reaches 743 verified substitutions where TensorFlow r1.14 "includes 155 substitutions implemented in approximately 53K lines of C++ code". Tiler answers this question differently and has already recorded the answer: rule authority is operation-owned, a rewrite names the effective numerical permission it consumes (ADR 0011), and [ADR 0095](../../decisions/0095-decline-a-distributivity-permission.md) declines a permission nothing needed rather than admitting a rewrite the contract could not justify. TASO's generate-then-verify loop is therefore *not* a model Tiler should copy — it produces substitutions whose soundness is proved against operator algebra alone, with no numerical-contract dimension at all, which is exactly the dimension Tiler will not give up.

**SPORES (`spores-pvldb-13-11-2020`)** applies e-graph search to linear algebra with an explicit ILP extractor and a greedy fallback. It is preserved partly as a correction: it makes no NP-hardness claim, and a reader who assumed it did would attribute the extraction-complexity result to the wrong paper.

**Extraction complexity.** Optimal e-graph extraction is NP-hard, and — the stronger and more consequential half — hard to approximate to any constant factor. **NP-hardness is read.** `stepp-thesis-ucsd-2011` establishes it in 2011 by reduction from Min-SAT: its chapter 8 carries "The MIN-SAT Problem", "NP-Hardness of the PEG Selection Problem", and reductions of PEG selection to Pseudo-Boolean and of stateful PEG selection to ILP.

**Correction — 2026-08-05: constant-factor inapproximability is now read too, and the qualification it carries was missing.** Until this date this paragraph read "Constant-factor inapproximability is relayed, not read", because the claim travelled only through `egraph-circuits-arxiv-2408.17042v2` citing a paper this survey could not retrieve. `sparse-extraction-oopsla-2024` has now been acquired and read at the proof. Its Section 3 proves by reduction from Set Cover that "there is no polynomial-time algorithm to approximate the Optimal Extraction problem within any constant approximation factor" **unless P=NP** — a qualification the relayed form of this record's sentence did not carry — and adds that "our proof stands even for a simple cost function that assigns costs of 0 or 1 to all vertices", which is what stops the result from being an artifact of an adversarial cost model. The relayed attribution was accurate in both directions: the citing paper reports the result correctly, and the cited paper in turn names it as independent concurrent work on the same FPT idea. **Elimination B's reason 2 is therefore fully read as of this date**, and Part 9's accounting is updated with it.

This is not an efficiency footnote for Tiler. The optimizer contract's own definition of "optimal" is the lowest-cost *valid* plan under the numerical contract and target profile, and a compiler that answers with a greedy extraction from a saturated e-graph is answering a different question than the one it documents. NP-hardness alone establishes that an exact extractor is not a bounded-time component; the inapproximability half is what makes an *approximate* extractor unable to carry a stated bound instead.

**The same paper carries a positive result that was flagged as possibly weakening this, and reading it settles that it does not.** `sparse-extraction-oopsla-2024` gives the first parameterized algorithm for optimal extraction: linear in e-graph size when treewidth is bounded, at `O(n · 5^k · k)` for tree decompositions of width `k` and `O(n · 3^k · k²)` for path decompositions, comfortably beating ILP on the 99% of Cranelift's benchmark e-graphs whose treewidth is at most 10. Three of the paper's own statements keep it away from Tiler's case, and they are its own limitations section rather than this record's objections. **First, it does not cover the extraction Tiler would need:** "Applications using Egg often requires acyclic extraction which is not covered by this work", and the acyclic generalization "causes the runtime to increase dramatically, leading to a bound of `O(c^(k²) · k · n)`. This is not very practical even for graphs with small treewidth." Acyclicity is not optional here — Tensat records that valid tensor rewrites routinely make the e-graph cyclic, and Tiler's region legality rests on DAG convexity. **Second, the sparsity does not generalize to saturation workloads:** "we found that benchmarks in Egg, i.e. in the extraction-gym benchmark, often have a large treewidth, so our method is not yet applicable to Egg benchmarks." **Third, the measured population is not comparable in scale:** Cranelift's ægraphs are per-function and deliberately acyclic, and the paper's Table 1 puts 252 255 of roughly 281 000 of them at 11–50 vertices with only 63 above 1 000 — against the 4 M e-nodes the guided-saturation measurement below reaches. The honest summary is that optimal extraction is tractable for small acyclic dataflow e-graphs built by a compiler that already restricted its e-graph design to make that true, which is a real result and is not Tiler's situation.

**Guided equality saturation (`guided-eqsat-popl-2024`) is the decisive measurement, because its search space is the one Tiler would actually be searching.** Its RISE case study takes seven increasingly complex matrix-multiplication optimization goals — baseline, blocking (tiling), vectorization, loop permutation, array packing, cache blocking, and multithreading — and expresses each as a rewrite target. Unguided equality saturation reaches two of the seven. `blocking` takes more than an hour and about 35 GB, applying about 5 M rules over an e-graph of about 4 M e-nodes and 2 M e-classes. The other five exhaust 60 GB of RAM without finding the goal. With at most three human-supplied *sketch guides*, every goal is found in under ten seconds using under 0.5 GB, over e-graphs of order 10⁴ — three orders of magnitude smaller.

**Measurement boundary.** Those figures come from a Scala engine "inspired by egg" rather than egg itself, with the rule scheduler deliberately disabled, on an Intel Xeon E5-2640 v2 with 60 GB available to the JVM, over RISE's lambda-calculus encoding. They bound that configuration. They are not proof that a Rust egg-based saturation over a different encoding fails at the same point, and this record does not claim they are.

**One qualification runs the other way, and it strengthens the evidence.** The obvious rebuttal to the numbers above is that the encoding was bad. `sketch-guided-eqsat-arxiv-2111.13040v2` — the same group's predecessor paper — reports that before its work "there are only naive encodings of the lambda calculus for equality saturation", and that its efficient encoding "reduces the runtime and memory consumption of equality saturation by orders of magnitude". The POPL 2024 tables are labelled "with efficient lambda calculus encoding". So the five failures at 60 GB are what remains *after* an orders-of-magnitude encoding improvement, not what an unoptimized encoding costs. A rebuttal from encoding quality now has to beat an encoding that was itself the subject of a paper.

**The hand-written baseline is now read, and it anchors the question "how much search is worth buying" that this record could previously only name.** `elevate-icfp-2020` is the strategy language the guided-saturation paper compares its rule counts against, over the *same* RISE array language and the *same* seven matrix-multiplication goals. Its Section 6.2 reports 657 rewrite steps for the baseline version, "about 40,000 steps for the next three versions and about 63,000 for the most complicated optimizations", and — the comparable quantity — that "applying the strategies to the RISE expression took less than two seconds per version on a commodity notebook with our unoptimized implementation", producing code that "performs competitively with TVM" at "about 110×" over the unoptimized baseline. **Counting boundary:** Elevate counts traversals as rewrite steps, so its step counts are not interchangeable with an e-graph's rule applications without that adjustment; the wall-clock figure is.

**Put the three routes on one scale and the shape of the trade is legible.** For the same seven goals: a hand-written Elevate strategy per goal reaches every one in under two seconds, at the cost of a human writing a strategy program; guided saturation reaches every one in under ten seconds under 0.5 GB, at the cost of a human writing at most three sketches; unguided saturation reaches two of seven and exhausts 60 GB on the rest. So automation here buys roughly a 5× wall-clock cost and replaces a strategy program with three sketches — it does *not* buy the removal of the human, and the published record contains no configuration in which it does. That bears directly on the cost-model thread's eventual question, and it bears on this record's own deferral: the value of a stage-1 e-graph is compact retention of alternatives, not the elimination of authored guidance.

**Inference, and it is the one the elimination rests on.** Tiler's rewrite space is not going to stay algebraic. The [flash-class capability derivation](../../../tickets/derive-the-capability-set-for-search-discovered-flash-class-attention-kernels.md) decomposes its target into a materialization decision, a streaming schedule, and an algebraic rewrite of the softmax normalization — and the first two are exactly the *kind* of choice RISE encoded as rewrite rules and blew past 60 GB on. The published result is not "e-graphs are too big for tensor programs"; Tensat contradicts that for pure graph substitutions. It is narrower and more useful: **an e-graph holding an algebraic rewrite space stays tractable, and an e-graph holding a schedule space does not.** Tiler's architecture already keeps those two spaces in different structures. Choosing saturation as the whole search would be choosing to put them in one.

## Part 3 — tensor-compiler schedule search

**Relevance question.** What did Halide and TVM/Ansor search over, and is their split — rewrite space and schedule space searched by different machinery — the right factoring here?

**Halide (`halide-autoscheduler-siggraph-2016`, `halide-autoscheduler-siggraph-2019`).** The 2019 paper opens on the language's defining property: "it separates the algorithm — what you want to compute — from the schedule — how you want to compute it". Its autoscheduler then searches *only the schedule*, with beam search over a schedule parameterization and a cost model learned from hundreds of thousands of random programs and schedules. The 2016 predecessor searched a smaller schedule space by *grouping*, which "seeks to identify points in the program where it is beneficial to restructure computation ordering to improve producer-consumer locality", using "a greedy, agglomerative" algorithm that repeatedly selects "the merge that yields the greatest performance benefit". **Neither searches algebraic rewrites at all.** The algorithm the user wrote is the algorithm that gets compiled.

That 2016 grouping algorithm is worth naming precisely rather than in passing, because it is the closest thing in the literature to what Tiler's cover enumeration would become if it were cost-driven: greedy, agglomerative, and committing to the best local merge. Tiler's cover search is deliberately not that — it is a budgeted exhaustive partition search checked against an oracle — and Part 4's witness is the demonstration of what greedy agglomeration costs.

**TVM and Ansor (`tvm-arxiv-1802.04799v3`, `ansor-arxiv-2006.06762v5`).** TVM adds an "ML-based cost model" over a schedule space defined through a "schedule template specification API", with the paper noting that a template is written per hardware back-end. Ansor's contribution is to remove the templates: a hierarchical space with "two levels: sketch and annotation", defining "the high-level structures of programs as sketches" and leaving "billions of low-level choices (e.g., tile size, parallel, unroll annotations) as annotations", explicitly to escape the "restricted search space and ineffective exploration strategy" of template-guided predecessors.

**Inference — the split is real, and Tiler already has it, in a different place.** Every system in this lineage separates a space searched by structural enumeration from a space searched by parameter sampling under a learned cost model. Tiler's two-level split is not algorithm-versus-schedule; it is **semantic candidates versus physical plans**, and the physical level splits again into cover enumeration and per-region implementation frontiers. Region formation and cover enumeration are Ansor's sketch level: structural, exhaustively enumerated up to budgets, and checked against an oracle. The implementation frontier is Ansor's annotation level, except that Tiler prunes it by typed feasibility and Pareto dominance rather than by a learned model, because no cost model exists.

The consequence for formalism choice is a caution rather than a lesson. This lineage does not answer the rewrite-search question because it does not ask it: none of these systems has an algebraic rewrite space, so none of them has ever had to compose one with a schedule space. Their evidence supports keeping the two spaces separate; it supplies nothing about how to search the algebraic one, which is why Part 2's corpus is the one that decides.

## Part 4 — phase ordering, against the attention worked example

**Relevance question.** Which orderings hide which compositions, stated precisely?

**Vocabulary (`whitfield-soffa-toplas-1997`).** Transformations "interact with one another by creating or destroying the potential for further" transformation; the paper derives *enabling* and *disabling* relations analytically from specifications rather than measuring them. This record uses that vocabulary deliberately, because "hides a candidate" is imprecise where "enables" and "disables" are not.

**The argument (`click-cooper-toplas-1995`).** Combining two analyses discovers facts neither discovers alone: "Combining these phases can lead to the discovery of more facts about the program, exposing more opportunities for optimization." Equality saturation is the same argument made about rewrites rather than analyses, twenty years later.

**The bound (`touati-barthou-cf-2006`).** Finding a phase sequence that yields an optimal program is *undecidable* in two general schemes of optimizing compilation, iterative compilation and library optimization. This bounds what any formalism may promise, and it is the reason the optimizer contract's definition of "optimal" is the right one: lowest-cost valid plan over an *enumerated candidate set*, under a stated target profile and numerical contract. No formalism selected here can raise that to a universal claim, and one that appeared to would be overclaiming rather than better.

### The worked example

The naive attention chain, written as registered semantic operations with no flash vocabulary anywhere:

```text
S = contraction(Q, Kᵀ)        # tiler::strict-tensor-contraction-f32@1
                              # (key spelling at this record's date; ADR 0112 renamed it tiler::tensor-contraction-f32@1)
P = softmax(S)                # tiler::softmax-f32@1, a max-fold then a sum-fold
O = contraction(P, V)
```

Two rewrite classes stand between this and a flash-class implementation, and they are owned by different parts of the compiler.

- **Algebraic:** state softmax's shifted-max rescaling identity, converting a *global* fold — one that reads every contributor before emitting its first result — into an *online* fold carrying a running maximum and a running sum with a rescale. Owned by the softmax family's declared algebraic capabilities, and consuming a numerical permission that does not exist yet.
- **Physical:** drop the materialization edge for `S`, so the quadratic score matrix is never a whole value. Owned by cover enumeration, which already proposes per-edge materialization choices.

**The enabling relation is mutual, and each half fails for a different reason.**

- *Physical-first fails on legality.* A global fold cannot run without all of `S` existing, so the no-`S` cover is not a cheaper program — it is not a program. A region containing the fold and no materialized input is refused.
- *Algebraic-first fails on cost.* On a program that still materializes `S`, the online fold is strictly more arithmetic for an identical result. Any rewriter that keeps a proposal only when it does not regress discards it, and the physical class never sees the form it needed.

**The composition is legal and profitable, and no single step reaches it.** That is the shape of the hazard, and it is not hypothetical for Tiler: the compiler's current stage-3 exploration is baseline-preserving precisely so that a proposal is not discarded for looking worse, and this is the concrete case that discipline exists for.

### The executable witness, and what it does and does not show

[`spikes/region-search/phase_ordering_witness.py`](../../../spikes/region-search/phase_ordering_witness.py) models exactly the structure above — four states over `(fold, materialize)`, a legality predicate, ordinal weights, and the two rewrite classes — and runs four strategies over it. Its result is sharper than the ticket's framing and is the reason it was worth writing:

| strategy | reaches the flash state? |
| --- | --- |
| greedy, phase-ordered (either of the two orders) | no |
| greedy, both classes interleaved to a fixpoint | no |
| retain alternatives, but discard any a cheaper predecessor dominates | no |
| retain every legal reachable alternative, choose at the end | **yes** |

The third row is the finding. Interleaving phases to a fixed point is the standard repair for phase ordering and it does not help, because the obstacle is not the order — it is that a cost comparison is consulted before the composition is complete. And *retention is not sufficient either*: a search that retains alternatives but prunes each one locally against a cheaper sibling behaves exactly like the greedy rewriter, because the flash-enabling intermediate is precisely the alternative local cost removes.

**Boundary — stated because the model is small enough to over-read.** This is a model of an ordering structure, not of Tiler's IR. It reads no `SemanticProgram`, computes no real cost, and proves no rewrite sound; the weights are ordinal and chosen to encode the enabling relation. It establishes that the phase-ordering hazard **separates cost-pruning search from alternative-retaining search**, and it deliberately establishes nothing that separates a Cascades memo from an e-graph — both retain the intermediate and both reach the composition, which is visible in the model as the single "retain every legal reachable alternative" row that covers them jointly. The witnesses were watched failing on three perturbations before this record was written: removing the legality half of the enabling relation, removing the cost half, and weakening the pruned-frontier strategy into full retention each turn a passing run red with a distinct named failure.

### The measured instance beside the model

**`metaflow-mlsys-2019`, acquired and read 2026-08-05, is the published system closest to the third row of that table, and it converts the finding from modelled to measured.** Its search enqueues a rewritten graph under `if Cost(G') < α × Cost(G_opt)`, and the paper states the degenerate case in its own words: "by setting α = 1, the search algorithm becomes a simple greedy algorithm and only considers graph substitutions that strictly reduce cost. As α increases, the search algorithm explores a larger part of the search space." Its motivating example is an enabling relation across a cost regression on a real network — a substitution that enlarges a convolution kernel from 1×1 to 3×3, which "downgrades runtime performance (since a convolution with a larger kernel runs slower) but enables additional subsequent kernel fusions", ending 1.3× faster than where it started. That is the attention example's shape, measured on ResNet rather than modelled with ordinal weights, and it is independent of this record.

**The qualification runs against this record and is recorded at full strength.** MetaFlow does not retain every legal alternative. It retains inside a bounded cost relaxation — default α = 1.05 — and that sufficed at its scale: its Table 3 reports the α = 1.05 search finding "the same optimal graph" as an exhaustive search on AlexNet, VGG16, an Inception module, and ResNet18, with ResNet18's search falling from 3.1 hours to 0.99 seconds. So a reader may fairly ask whether this record's absolute "no semantic alternative is pruned on estimated cost" is stronger than the evidence requires, when 5% of slack recovered the exhaustive optimum on four networks. **Two things answer that, and neither is a preference.** First, MetaFlow's α cannot express the *legality* half of Part 4's enabling relation at all: a global fold with no materialized input is not a costly program, it is not a program, and no relaxation factor reaches it. Second, MetaFlow demonstrates the collapse this record's contract forbids, in its own cost model: it handles a hard resource limit by "returning an infinite cost if the memory usage limit is exceeded". That is a feasibility refusal expressed as a cost, in the closest published system to Tiler's own finding — which is the best available evidence that a search with one retention authority will reach for that substitution, because it is the only place it has to put the answer. The invariant survives the qualification; what the qualification correctly removes is any claim that full retention is the *only* strategy that ever works, which this record should not have been read as making.

**Governance note, because this qualification is the most minable paragraph in the record.** The no-cost-pruning invariant is contract text rather than a proposal — Part 8 records the carrier that landed it — so MetaFlow's α is evidence to be weighed in an explicit relaxation proposal, and is not itself one. Anyone reaching for it should carry the two answers above with it, and in particular the second: the system being cited as the precedent for bounded cost relaxation is the same system that expressed a hard resource limit as an infinite cost.

**MetaFlow's own boundary.** Its substitutions are semantics-preserving graph substitutions with no numerical-contract dimension at all — the same gap as its sibling TASO, from the same group. Its α is a tuned hyperparameter carrying no bound, and its agreement with exhaustive search is a measured result on four networks under one cost model, not a guarantee. It is evidence about the enabling relation and about the infinite-cost collapse; it is not evidence that any particular slack is safe.

## Part 5 — the elimination

Four candidate formalisms. Each is stated with what would refute its elimination, so a reader can attack the derivation rather than only the conclusion.

### A. Destructive, cost-pruned rewriting to a fixpoint

**Eliminated.** It cannot reach a mutually-enabled composition, by Part 4's witness, and iterating to a fixed point does not repair it. Independently, it forfeits the explain obligation: a destructive rewriter that discarded a proposal has no candidate to attribute a rejection to.

*Refutable by:* showing that the flash-class composition (or any composition Tiler intends to discover) decomposes into steps each of which is individually non-regressing under some admissible cost model. That would make the enabling relation an artifact of the modelled weights rather than a property of the target. Note that it would have to hold for the *legality* half too, which no cost model can repair — a global fold with no materialized input is not a costly program, it is not a program.

### B. Equality saturation as the whole search

**Eliminated as the search.** Four independent reasons, any one of which is enough:

1. **The published tractability evidence is against it for the space Tiler is heading into.** `guided-eqsat-popl-2024`: unguided saturation over a *schedule* rewrite space failed five of seven goals at 60 GB, and needed human sketches to succeed. Tiler's next rewrite space is exactly that kind. Tensat's success on pure algebraic graph substitutions is real and does not transfer to the composed space.
2. **Extraction is NP-hard and constant-factor inapproximable, and as of 2026-08-05 both halves are read at their proofs** — NP-hardness in `stepp-thesis-ucsd-2011` chapter 8, inapproximability in `sparse-extraction-oopsla-2024` Section 3, by reduction from Set Cover and holding even for a 0/1 cost function, unless P=NP. (Until that date the second half was relayed through `egraph-circuits-arxiv-2408.17042v2`; Part 2 carries the correction.) A greedy extractor answers a different question than the contract documents, with no stated bound. An exact ILP extractor makes compile time a solver's runtime, which is not a deterministic budget. **The acquisition was flagged as possibly weakening this reason and did the opposite.** The same paper's fast optimal algorithm for sparse e-graphs is FPT in treewidth, excludes the acyclic extraction Tiler would need ("not covered by this work", and the acyclic generalization is "not very practical even for graphs with small treewidth"), and reports that egg's own benchmark e-graphs "often have a large treewidth, so our method is not yet applicable". Its sparsity was measured on Cranelift's deliberately acyclic per-function ægraphs, most of which have between 11 and 50 vertices.
3. **Saturation and hard feasibility are in tension — and this reason is weaker than it first looks, so it is stated at the strength the evidence supports.** An e-graph asserts that everything in an e-class is interchangeable. Tiler's alternatives are not interchangeable: one may be infeasible on a target, or impermissible under the caller's numerical contract, and both are *typed refusals with reasons*, not costs. Pushing feasibility into the extractor's cost function is exactly the substitution [ADR 0043](../../decisions/0043-use-typed-phased-target-feasibility.md) forbids, and that half stands unqualified.

   **The other half — that contract-dependent e-class membership means one e-graph per resolved numerical contract — is partially refuted by the literature, and the refutation was found after this reason was first written.** `colored-egraphs-fmcad-2024` makes exactly the observation this record was relying on being expensive: "each added assumption, can be treated as a new congruence relation, but is only a coarsening of the original relation" (*sic*, the stray comma is the paper's), and a colored e-graph is "a memory-efficient equivalent of multiple copies of an e-graph, with a much lower overhead", achieved by sharing structure while "carefully tracking which conclusion is true under which assumption". A numerical contract is an assumption of exactly that shape. So the honest version of this reason is: representing several contracts in one e-graph is a solved problem in principle *for assumptions that coarsen modestly*, at a cost measured on theory-exploration workloads and never on a compiler; what is *not* solved is expressing a typed refusal with a reason, because a colored e-graph records which conclusions hold under which assumption and not why a conclusion was refused. Reason 3 therefore survives on the refusal half alone, and the record says so rather than keeping the sentence the evidence contradicts.

*Refutable by:* a measurement showing an egg-based (or egglog-based) saturation over Tiler's *actual* rewrite vocabulary, including whatever the flash capability set adds, staying inside a stated node and time budget on the attention program — plus a stated extraction contract that is either exact within a deterministic budget or explicitly approximate with a named bound. Part 6 scopes that experiment.

### C. A Cascades-style memo as the whole search, over one algebra

**Eliminated, and this is the elimination most likely to surprise.** Cascades is the closest fit and its mechanisms transfer better than anything else in the corpus. What it cannot carry is Tiler's *ordering of three pruning authorities*. Cascades has one retention mechanism — cost, with a branch-and-bound limit — and prunes with `promise` functions whose own paper says an incorrect one causes "incorrect pruning of the search space". Tiler's contract requires numerical-contract preference to prune *before* physical planning, feasibility to refuse with a reason and never a cost, and estimated cost to act only last and only inside one contract group. A single memo keyed on `(group, required physical properties, cost limit)` has nowhere to put the first two without turning them into properties, and a numerical contract is not a physical property: it changes what the program *means*, which is why the contract states that "no cheaper plan under a different contract can buy a change in meaning".

**The strongest objection to this elimination is Orca, and it is answered rather than ignored.** `orca-sigmod-2014` shows a production Cascades whose property-enforcement framework is genuinely extensible from "formal property specifications" — sort order, distribution, output columns, rewindability, common table expressions, data partitioning — with each operator controlling enforcer placement. If a property framework can absorb data partitioning across a distributed cluster, why not a numerical contract? Because every property in Orca's list answers *how a result is delivered*, and satisfaction is delivery-or-enforce: a request that cannot be met directly is met by plugging in an enforcer, and "it is up to the cost model to differentiate their costs". A numerical contract has no enforcer — nothing can be plugged into a plan to convert a strict-`f32` result into a reassociated one, because the two are different values — and it has no cost, because preferring one is preferring a different meaning. Orca demonstrates that the framework generalizes across delivery properties, which is the axis Tiler already borrowed from Volcano and Selinger. It demonstrates nothing about an authority that is neither enforceable nor costable, and that is precisely the authority Tiler's contract preference is.

Note also that Cascades' unified memo is an answer to a problem Tiler does not have. Cascades merged logical and physical operators into one algebra because predicate manipulation was "very cumbersome" as an operator argument in Volcano. Tiler's semantic/physical separation is an architectural invariant with its own accepted decisions behind it; adopting the unification would be paying Cascades' price for a benefit Tiler's IR already provides another way.

**The measured Cascades reimplementation, read 2026-08-05, does not move this verdict and sharpens what it costs.** `columbia-optimizer-thesis-1998` is where Cascades' proposed search-efficiency techniques were actually measured, and its two techniques have different standing in a way that matters here. *Lower-bound group pruning* is safe — the thesis runs its headline results with "only lower bound group pruning in Columbia to demonstrate the best performance of Columbia while still generates optimal solutions" — and under it a 16-table chain query optimizes in under a minute in under 150 MB, while star queries degrade fast enough that the thesis' own conclusion records memory becoming "prohibitive when optimizing very large queries, such as the star queries with greater than 16 tables". That is the calibration this record previously said it did not have. *Global epsilon pruning*, the second technique, buys its further reduction by giving up optimality, and the thesis measures the exchange rate on an 8-table chain query: 3 174 multi-expressions at zero error, 1 293 at an error of 0.4, and 608 at an error of 1.0 — where its own definition makes an error of 1 mean "the cost of the optimizer output is twice as much as the optimal plan". **The elimination above is not on efficiency grounds and does not become one.** What Columbia adds is that the memo's scale cost in the one system that measured it was paid down with a knob Tiler's contract forbids — the same axis as Orca's cost-threshold stage termination, now with an error curve attached to it. **Measurement-validity caveat:** the thesis' Columbia-versus-Cascades figures run the two optimizers on different machines and operating systems (its own footnote 18), so the trend is suggestive and the ratios are not a controlled measurement.

*Refutable by:* an encoding of the numerical contract and typed feasibility as memo *properties* that preserves both the ordering (contract preference strictly before cost) and the typed refusal reasons — that is, a demonstration that a three-authority ordering is expressible in a single property-keyed memo without collapsing a refusal into a cost. This record could not construct one; that is an argument from failure to find, not a proof of impossibility, and it is the weakest step in this elimination.

### D. Staged, alternative-retaining search — the survivor

**Selected.** The formalism is a *staged* search in which each stage retains every legal alternative its rules propose, no stage prunes a semantic alternative on estimated cost, and each stage's retention authority is the one appropriate to what it decides:

1. **Semantic exploration** proposes contract-preserving alternatives from operation-owned rules and retains all of them alongside an unconditionally preserved baseline. Its refusals are semantic-applicability, numerical-permission, and configuration declines with distinct typed assessments — never a cost.
2. **Contract grouping** partitions candidates by resolved numerical contract and evaluates groups in the caller's stated preference order, so a later group is preference-pruned without physical planning.
3. **Physical enumeration** — region formation, cover enumeration, per-region implementation frontiers — is the memoized level. Its memo is keyed on physical state (today: coverage masks) and its pruning is typed feasibility followed by Pareto dominance within comparable applicability domains.
4. **Global selection** compares complete plans, and only inside one contract group.

**This is largely what the compiler already does**, which is a strength of the derivation rather than a weakness of it: the contract's obligations were written first, the implementation followed them, and the literature survey finds that the obligations already exclude every alternative formalism. What this record adds is threefold. It names the invariant that makes the staging work — **no semantic alternative is discarded on estimated cost, at any stage** — as a durable commitment rather than an implementation accident. It records that stage 1's retention is the *whole* mechanism against phase ordering, so a future optimization that adds a profitability check there would silently reintroduce the hazard Part 4 demonstrates. And it names what the staging owes and does not yet have: stage 1 currently retains at most three whole-program proposals, and a rewrite vocabulary rich enough for flash-class discovery will need a real alternative structure at that stage, which is where equality saturation returns as a *component*.

**Where equality saturation is the right component, with its trigger.** Stage 1's job — hold many equivalent semantic forms compactly, apply local rewrites without choosing between them, and answer "is this form present" — is precisely what an e-graph is good at, and it is *not* the schedule space that broke RISE. If stage 1's rewrite vocabulary grows past roughly a dozen rules, or if any rule becomes non-terminating under repeated application (associativity and commutativity both are), the whole-program-proposal-per-rule structure stops working and an e-graph over the semantic algebra alone becomes the right representation, with extraction replaced by *enumeration into stage 2* rather than by a cost-based extractor. That keeps every reason B was eliminated on the outside: no schedule space in the e-graph, no cost-based extraction, no feasibility in an e-class. The trigger is stated in the deferral below.

**Correction — 2026-08-05: the precedent this deferral was expected to lean on does not exist in the form this record described it.** Part 9 listed `denali-pldi-2002` as goal-directed rather than saturating use of an e-graph, and said acquiring it "would strengthen the record's deferral". Reading it shows the opposite structure: Denali saturates its E-graph to quiescence and puts the goal-direction in a SAT-refuted cycle-bound conjecture over the saturated result. **The deferral is unaffected because it never rested on Denali** — it rests on what stage 1's job is, and on the three exclusions listed above — but the sentence claiming a twenty-year-old precedent for goal-directed *exploration* is withdrawn. What Denali does supply is a caution for the same deferral, and it is worth reading beside the trigger: at a 31-instruction loop body, Denali's saturation phase dominated its selection phase by more than two orders of magnitude in wall time, and its authors named the matcher's termination heuristics as the first reason its output is "near-optimal" rather than optimal. A stage-1 e-graph that saturates over a growing vocabulary inherits both properties, and the probe in Part 7 is where they would be measured rather than assumed.

## Part 6 — mapping Tiler's obligations onto the selected structures

The optimizer contract's four obligations, each answered against the survivor and, where the literature answers differently, against equality saturation as the contrast.

**Typed identities.** Each retained alternative carries an identity re-derived from the owning rule origin, the exact semantic program, the exact verified request, and the physical plan; a mismatched owner key, missing or extra alternative, forged winner, or identity derived from another candidate is invalid compiler output. This survives staging because each stage's alternatives are distinct objects with provenance. **It is exactly what a saturated e-graph destroys.** In an e-graph the derivation is not retained — that is the point of congruence closure, and it is why egg needed a separate proof-production mechanism to answer "why are these equal". A component-level e-graph at stage 1 must therefore either retain per-e-node rule provenance or produce an explanation on extraction; this is a real obligation on the deferred adoption, not a detail.

**Explain for accepted *and rejected* candidates.** The contract requires every rejection to record its stage, stable reason code, rule or provider identity, affected subject, failed predicate, and whether the result is a hard rejection, safe deferral, budget stop, dominance pruning, or cost disadvantage — and forbids collapsing these into "not fused". The staged formalism satisfies this because every stage's refusal is a typed assessment on a named candidate.

**What explain means for a saturated e-graph is the sharpest question in the ticket, and the honest answer is that the two notions of "rejected" are different.** A saturated e-graph's positive claim is *"every form derivable from these rules is present"* — it has no rejections to report, because nothing was refused; forms are absent only because no rule derived them. A *budgeted* e-graph, which is what every practical run is (`egg`'s own loop is `is_saturated_or_timeout`), has a third category that is neither present nor underivable: **not yet derived when the budget stopped**, and it cannot enumerate that category, because enumerating what a search did not reach is the search. This is not a solvable engineering problem; it is a property of the representation. The contract's rule that "a budget stop is the one disposition that says nothing about its subject, so it never stands alone" is satisfiable for a stage that stopped a named growth path on a named subject, and is *not* satisfiable for a saturation run that stopped globally. A component-level e-graph at stage 1 must therefore report its budget stop as a stop on the *stage*, with an `Unknown` evidence class over the stage's whole output, rather than pretending to a per-candidate account it cannot produce.

**Deterministic budgets.** Tiler's fourteen deterministic budgets are four classes, not one, and only one of the four matches the sentence a formalism comparison wants — that exhausting a budget costs an alternative while complete coverage survives. **Five bound a submitted *program*** (`semantic_values`, `semantic_operations`, `regions`, `host_expression_nodes`, `buffers`) and refuse it before any search runs at all. **Three bound one region's admissible *shape*** (`region_members`, `region_boundary_outputs`, `region_live_values`): they declare the largest region the profile forms, so a program whose only implementable cover needs a bigger one has no plan under them however long the search runs. **Five are genuinely alternative-preserving search caps** (`normalization_rewrites: 8`, `region_candidates_per_seed`, `region_expansions`, `region_covers`, `region_cover_expansions`), and what makes them so is that each owning stage retains an unbounded result before its search begins: the verified input, both extremes of the partition lattice, and the fully-materialized and fused covers. **The fourteenth, `physical_plan_combinations`, is a search cap with no such retained result** — complete-plan selection is the first authority that proves cross-region boundary compatibility rather than coverage, so exhausting it can leave the portfolio empty; Tom accepted that contract on 2026-08-11 through [`state-the-rule-that-a-deterministic-budget-is-a-derivation`](../../../tickets/state-the-rule-that-a-deterministic-budget-is-a-derivation.md). All fourteen are attributable, which is the property this comparison actually rests on: each names which growth path stopped, on which subject, with the exact limit and demand. In the staged formalism a new budget is added at the stage that owns the growth it bounds, and it must say which of these four classes it joins.

**Correction — 2026-08-18: the paragraph above previously asserted one class for all of them, and the assertion was wrong in two independent ways.** The retired wording opened `Tiler's budgets bound *a search*, so exhausting one costs an alternative while complete coverage survives` and counted `five` `region_*` fields in that class; a grep landing on either fragment has found this note quoting withdrawn text, not a live claim. Three of those five are region-shape bounds that can refuse a program outright, which [the optimizer contract](../../compiler/optimizer.md) and `DeterministicBudgets`' own field documentation in `crates/tiler-compiler/src/request.rs` both state; and `physical_plan_combinations` is a search cap whose exhaustion can remove every complete plan rather than one alternative. Neither repair changes what this record concluded about e-graphs — the elimination rests on a budget being attributable to a named growth path on a named subject, and all fourteen still are — but it sharpens the contrast in the direction that strengthens it, because a global node-, e-class-, or iteration-count budget cannot express any of the four classes, let alone tell them apart.

**The DB lineage's answer is Orca's multi-stage optimization, and the gap between it and Tiler's is instructive.** An Orca stage is "a complete optimization workflow using a subset of transformation rules and (optional) time-out and cost threshold", terminating on cost-threshold satisfaction, time-out, or rule-subset exhaustion, so that "the most expensive transformation rules are configured to run in later stages to avoid increasing the optimization time". Two of those three terminations are non-deterministic in the sense Tiler cares about: a wall-clock time-out makes the output a function of machine load, and a cost threshold makes it a function of how quickly a good-enough plan happened to be found. Tiler's budgets are counts of *work performed*, which is why the same request compiles to the same portfolio twice. Orca is therefore evidence that rule-subset staging is a workable budget axis Tiler does not yet have — a stage that runs only the cheap rules first is compatible with deterministic counting — and evidence that the other two axes are the ones to keep out.

**The cost-threshold axis now has a measured error curve attached to it, which is the part Orca does not supply.** `columbia-optimizer-thesis-1998`'s global epsilon pruning is the same axis in an earlier system, and unlike Orca it reports what the termination costs the answer: on an 8-table chain query, epsilon 0 gives the optimal plan at 3 174 multi-expressions, epsilon 15 gives 1 293 at an error of 0.4, and epsilon 30 gives 608 at an error of 1.0 — an output costing twice the optimal plan. Read beside Columbia's other technique, the point is sharper than "cost thresholds are non-deterministic": the *safe* pruning (lower-bound group pruning, which the thesis states preserves optimal solutions) was the one that carried its headline chain-query result, and the additional order of reduction came from the lossy one. A budget axis that is a count of work performed and a budget axis that is a quality threshold are different objects, and this is the corpus' only measurement of the price of confusing them.

**What a deterministic budget bounds in an e-graph is not the same kind of quantity**, and this is the second reason B was eliminated. The natural e-graph budgets are node count, e-class count, and iteration count, and all three are *global* — they bound the representation, not a path through it. Two consequences follow. A node-count budget's effect depends on rule application order, so determinism has to be recovered by fixing the iteration order and the rule order, which is achievable but is a stronger requirement than the per-path budgets Tiler has today. And exhaustion is not attributable to a subject: the answer is "the e-graph got too big", which names no candidate and no growth path. Extraction budgets are worse, because an ILP solver's time limit yields a *suboptimal* answer with no bound, and the contract's "optimal" is defined over the enumerated candidate set.

**Hard feasibility separate from estimated cost.** The staged formalism keeps them in separate stages with no conversion between them, which is how the contract already enforces it. The e-graph contrast is stated in elimination B, reason 3, and is the reason a component-level adoption at stage 1 must sit strictly *before* contract grouping: an e-graph whose e-class membership depended on a resolved numerical contract would not be one structure but one per group.

**Fail closed with typed, explainable errors.** Unchanged by the choice. It is worth recording that this obligation is what makes the whole elimination possible: a system permitted to return an approximate answer would have far more formalisms available to it, and constant-factor-inapproximable extraction would be a cost rather than a disqualification.
