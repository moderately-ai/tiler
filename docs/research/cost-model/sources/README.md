# Preserved primary sources for the measured-feedback tuning-loop design

These records keep the autotuning and adaptive-execution literature behind [the measured-feedback tuning loop](../measured-feedback-tuning-loop.md) reproducible when an upstream URL moves, changes, or disappears. Preservation is licence-aware rather than uniform, on the discipline the [rewrite-search source record](../../region-search/sources/README.md) and the [numerics source record](../../numerics/sources/README.md) established: a document whose own terms permit dissemination is vendored byte-for-byte, and a document whose terms do not — or whose terms could not be read — keeps bibliographic identity, a retrieval fingerprint where one exists, and an official acquisition route, with no bytes checked in.

**Every record here is metadata-only, and none is vendored — and since 2026-08-07 that is the outcome of fourteen separate licence readings rather than a default, with no row left unread.** The distinction is the whole point of this paragraph, because the sentence that stood here before said the opposite: it declared the uniform classification "a *deliberate uniform classification and not a licence reading*", correctly, because no document had then been assessed. Each of the fourteen has now been assessed — the first ten under [`vendor-the-tuning-loop-primary-sources-after-reading-each-licence`](../../../../tickets/vendor-the-tuning-loop-primary-sources-after-reading-each-licence.md) and the last four under [`close-the-four-licence-readings-tom-supplied-and-admit-graefe-and-ward`](../../../../tickets/close-the-four-licence-readings-tom-supplied-and-admit-graefe-and-ward.md) — and **not one of them carries a grant this repository may redistribute under**. They fail for four distinguishable reasons worth keeping apart: **six** modern ACM notices refuse redistribution outright, naming "post on servers" among the refused acts; **three** arXiv submissions carry the default non-exclusive licence, which grants arXiv a distribution licence and a third party nothing; **one** — the *Foundations and Trends* survey — carries no grant at all, only a bare copyright line, so it fails on absence rather than refusal; and **four** do grant copying but condition it on the copies not being made or distributed for "direct commercial advantage", which this repository does not clearly satisfy. Only that last group is a judgement, and it is recorded as one so it can be revisited without re-reading the notices. **A reader must not read the unchanged bottom line as evidence that the reading was skipped.**

**The four conditional grants are exactly the four pre-modern notices, which is a fact about publishing eras rather than a coincidence.** Two are VLDB Endowment notices ([`pqo-vldb-1992`](#pqo-vldb-1992), [`plan-diagrams-vldb-2005`](#plan-diagrams-vldb-2005)) and two are ACM notices predating the "post on servers" phrasing ([`graefe-ward-sigmod-1989`](#graefe-ward-sigmod-1989), [`cole-graefe-sigmod-1994`](#cole-graefe-sigmod-1994)). All four carry the same operative sentence shape — permission to copy without fee, conditioned on the copies not being for direct commercial advantage, with republication reserved — and all four therefore resolve on one judgement rather than on four. A future reader who concludes that condition *is* satisfied for this repository moves all four rows together, and should; a reader who concludes it is not, as this record does, needs the judgement made once.

**The three arXiv rows were the reason this reading was commissioned, and reading them refuted the hypothesis behind it.** The ticket's stated inference was that AutoTVM, Ansor, and TVM are "arXiv submissions whose submission licence is plausibly permissive". All three carry the default `arXiv.org perpetual, non-exclusive license 1.0`, which grants arXiv a distribution licence and grants a third party nothing. None of the three abstract pages mentions Creative Commons at all, and none of the three PDFs carries any rights statement of its own. That is the single most common reason a paper is not vendorable in this repository, and the [rewrite-search record](../../region-search/sources/README.md) had already reached it independently for two of these same three documents on 2026-08-05 — see the overlap note below.

**Retrieval date for every reachable record below: 2026-08-07**, followed by `pdftotext` (Poppler) into the text actually read. The digest in each row is a SHA-256 over the exact acquired PDF byte stream. **Ten of those byte streams were read and then discarded. Four are retained on one machine under `local/`, gitignored and never redistributed**, on the [`local-only` classification](#the-local-only-classification) defined below; no bytes are checked in, here or anywhere. **Thirteen of the fourteen were retrieved by `curl` from the recorded URL on the coordination host.** The fourteenth, [`graefe-ward-sigmod-1989`](#graefe-ward-sigmod-1989), was **not fetched by this host at all** — it was supplied by Tom from a normal browser session after this host's every route to it terminated at a bot wall. Its provenance is recorded in its own row and must not be read as a retrieval this repository could reproduce.

**Licence-reading dates: 2026-08-07, in two passes.** Reading a licence needs the bytes, and this record retained none at the time, so every document was obtained again and **its digest compared against the one already recorded here before a word of its licence was read**. That re-acquisition cost is what the `local-only` arrangement exists to avoid paying a third time: three of the four documents obtained in the second pass could not be re-fetched by this host at all.

The **first pass** re-retrieved by `curl` and reached ten of the thirteen rows then recorded. All ten reproduced their recorded digest exactly. For the seven whose grant lives in the document, that means the clause quoted below was read in provably the same byte stream the design record's claims were checked against; for the three arXiv rows the grant is submission metadata and the reproduced digest instead confirms that the abstract page checked and the bytes digested describe one and the same version. The other three did not arrive at all, and were recorded as unread with what blocked them.

The **second pass** closed those three and admitted a fourth document. Tom supplied four PDFs from a normal browser session — the two `www.vldb.org` papers, the eScholarship deposit, and the ACM Digital Library copy of Graefe and Ward that had never been retrieved by anyone here. **The three with recorded digests were re-verified against this record before any terms were read, and all three reproduced exactly**: `401c6f66…b7b8` at 499,633 bytes, `c8713911…03da` at 1,293,795, and `e4dd35a0…79c0` at 3,845,934. So the clauses quoted below were read in the same byte streams the design record's claims were already checked against, not in substitutes. Graefe and Ward had no digest to reproduce because it had never been held; its digest is recorded for the first time in its own row.

**No digest in this record has ever changed, and none was rewritten to match a fresh acquisition** — an acquisition that produced different bytes would have been a finding, and none did.

**A manifest and a `verify-sources.sh` now accompany this record**, at [`expected-sources.tsv`](expected-sources.tsv) and [`verify-sources.sh`](verify-sources.sh), on the [numerics record's](../../numerics/sources/README.md) convention. The paragraph that stood here argued they would be vacuous, because zero bytes were vendored and no file sat beside this README, and recorded a mild disagreement: a manifest would still assert the declared population count and id uniqueness, which are not vacuous. **The `local-only` arrangement below settled that disagreement against the original decision**, and for a reason neither side had: a gitignored directory is invisible to `git status`, so it is exactly where a stray licence-restricted PDF sits unnoticed and unattributed. The check's file-escape sweep descends into `local/` precisely because git does not, and that sweep is now the strongest reason the manifest exists.

### The `local-only` classification

The manifest's fourth classification, added here and not present in the sibling records. **`local-only` means: the bytes are retained on one machine, at a gitignored path under `local/`, with the digest pinned in the manifest, and are deliberately never redistributed.** There is no link to that directory anywhere in this record, because on every machine but one there is nothing for the link to reach.

It is defined against its two neighbours, because collapsing it into either would assert something false:

- **It is not `vendored`.** Vendored bytes are checked in and travel with every clone, which is redistribution and which **all fourteen licence verdicts refuse**. Nothing under `local/` is in git history, no clone receives it, and the repository distributes none of it. Retaining a copy on the machine that read it is not publication in any sense the four notices reach — each of them governs copying and republication, and none is engaged by a reader keeping the document he read.
- **It is not `metadata-only`.** A `metadata-only` row asserts that no bytes are retained anywhere, so a reader wanting to check a quotation must re-acquire the document themselves. A `local-only` row asserts the opposite on one machine: the bytes are present and re-readable, so a claim can be re-checked here without a re-acquisition that a publisher may refuse a second time. Three of these four rows exist because exactly that re-acquisition failed.

**The licence verdict is unchanged by the classification, and the two columns must not be confused.** All fourteen rows are non-redistributable and the [licence table](#licence-and-verdict-2026-08-07) records `metadata-only` as the *verdict* for every one of them. The manifest's classification column is a **preservation** class, not a redistribution verdict, and the four `local-only` rows carry the same refusing verdict as the ten beside them. The manifest states this caution in its own header so a reader who opens only the TSV still meets it.

**The digest is what makes a `local-only` row meaningful on a machine that does not have the file** — which is every machine but one, including every fresh clone and every other worktree. The row is not a pointer to a file a reader is expected to find; it is the identity a re-acquisition is checked against, exactly as for a `metadata-only` row. That is why `verify-sources.sh` treats an absent `local-only` file as expected rather than as a failure, and why it nonetheless prints how many were found and how many were absent: a run that verified no bytes at all must not read like a run that verified them and found them good.

**Retained bytes are not evidence of retained permission.** If a future reader concludes any of these four documents may be vendored, the licence reading is what must change, in its row, with the notice re-read — not the preservation class. Moving a row from `local-only` to `vendored` is a redistribution decision requiring the four pre-modern notices to be re-judged together, as the opening section explains, and not merely a file to be `git add`ed.

## What these records claim, and what they do not

- **Retrieval fingerprint.** The recorded digest pins the exact byte stream that was read when the design record's claims about that document were checked. It is evidence that a re-acquired copy is or is not the same bytes. It is not permission, and its absence from this directory is deliberate. **It is also not a claim that this repository can obtain the document again**: thirteen rows were fetched by `curl` from their recorded URL and one, [`graefe-ward-sigmod-1989`](#graefe-ward-sigmod-1989), was supplied by Tom from a browser after this host was refused. Reproducibility of the *bytes* and reproducibility of the *acquisition* are separate properties and only the first is what a digest carries.
- **Licence verdict.** Each row states what its rights statement says, *where that statement was read*, and whether this repository may redistribute the bytes. The "where" is load-bearing and differs by row: a notice printed inside the document is verifiable from the document, a grant recorded as submission metadata is verifiable only at the publisher's page and not from the bytes at all, and one row's notice sits on page 2 because page 1 is a repository cover sheet. **No row currently reports an unread licence.** The convention for one that did is retained because it may be needed again: such a row is making a claim about this host on a date, not about the document's terms, and it is not a finding that the document is closed.
- **Quotation.** Every sentence [the design record](../measured-feedback-tuning-loop.md) quotes from one of these documents was read in the extracted text of the exact byte stream digested here, not in an abstract, a summary, or a secondary description. Where the design record could not verify a claim it says so and names the row.
- **Tiler inference.** Every decision, elimination, and mapping drawn from these documents lives in the design record, not here. Preserving a reference does not endorse its conclusions, and a measurement inside a paper stays bounded by that paper's own environment.
- **Distilled reading notes**, for the four documents read on 2026-08-07, live in [`../reading-notes/`](../reading-notes/): [`graefe-ward-sigmod-1989`](../reading-notes/graefe-ward-sigmod-1989.md), [`plan-diagrams-vldb-2005`](../reading-notes/plan-diagrams-vldb-2005.md), [`pqo-vldb-1992`](../reading-notes/pqo-vldb-1992.md), and [`halide-autoscheduler-2019`](../reading-notes/halide-autoscheduler-2019.md). They exist because these four could not be re-fetched on demand, so a note is the only durable substitute for re-reading bytes a clone does not have. **A note is not authority over its row or over the design record**: the row owns identity and licence, the design record owns every conclusion, and a note that disagrees with either is a defect in the note.

## Population boundary

This record closes over every document the design record cites as primary evidence, in four groups matching its own sections. A citation the design record adds later extends this record and restates the boundary here rather than starting a second manifest.

**Tensor-program autotuning** — AutoTVM, Ansor, TVM, and the two Halide autoschedulers. These carry the measured-search loop the design record's *where measurement enters* section is decided against: the measurement budget an operator-level tuner actually spends, the surrogate-model-plus-hardware-batch structure, the transfer mechanism and its stated invariance requirement, and — from the two Halide records read together — the one directly comparable pair of a no-benchmarking analytic scheduler and a learned one with optional ground-truth autotuning.

**General program autotuning** — OpenTuner. It is the group of one that carries a tuning *store* as an explicit architectural component rather than an implementation detail, which is the subject of the design record's identity-discipline section.

**Adaptive execution and parametric plans** — the adaptive-query-processing survey, Kabra and DeWitt's mid-query re-optimization, Ioannidis et al.'s parametric query optimization, Reddy and Haritsa's plan diagrams, Markl et al.'s progressive optimization, Graefe and Ward's dynamic query evaluation plans, and Cole and Graefe's optimization of them. These carry the twenty-year prior art on the shape-transfer question: what a plan's validity region is and how one is actually computed, when an observed parameter invalidates a cached plan, why keeping one plan per parameter point is refused, the measured geometry of how coarse the winner partition actually is, and the measured cost of the one design that ships every potentially optimal plan in a single artifact and picks among them later. **The last two are one line of work read at both ends**, five years apart, which is what makes each one's characterisation of the other checkable rather than merely quotable.

**Benchmarking statistics** — Hoefler and Belli. It is the source of the twelve rules the design record's statistical section is written against, including the two it records itself as failing.

### Four of these documents are also preserved under the rewrite-search record, and the two records agree

`ansor-osdi-2020`, `tvm-osdi-2018`, `halide-autoscheduler-2016`, and `halide-autoscheduler-2019` are the same four *documents* as [the rewrite-search record's](../../region-search/sources/README.md) `ansor-arxiv-2006.06762v5`, `tvm-arxiv-1802.04799v3`, `halide-autoscheduler-siggraph-2016`, and `halide-autoscheduler-siggraph-2019`. That record reached its licence verdicts on 2026-08-05, independently and over a different literature, and **this reading agrees with all four**: arXiv non-exclusive for the first two, ACM copyright for the other two, metadata-only in every case. Two of them are the same *bytes* as well — the digests for `ansor` and `tvm` match that record's rows exactly, as does `halide-autoscheduler-2016`, all three retrieved from the same URLs. `halide-autoscheduler-2019` is the one document held here as a **different byte stream**: this record's copy is eScholarship's (`e4dd35a0…`) and that record's is the Halide project's own (`8f9f173617…`). They are the same article under two hosts, and neither digest is evidence about the other.

**That last row is now the strongest of the four cross-checks, and it was the weakest.** Until 2026-08-07 this record had not read its own copy's notice and leaned on the sibling record's reading of a *different* copy, which it declared insufficient to vendor on. Both notices have now been read independently, in two different byte streams under two different hosts, and **they agree**: the same modern ACM permission block, the same "to post on servers" refusal, the same metadata-only verdict. Two copies of one article are not obliged to carry the same rights notice — that was the reason for the caution — and here they demonstrably do.

**Neither record is the authority for the other, and this note does not merge them.** The two populations are declared separately on purpose — each closes over the citations of its own design record — and a document cited by both is legitimately preserved by both. What the overlap buys is a cross-check: four verdicts reached twice, two days apart, by separate readings, with no disagreement. What it costs is a maintenance obligation worth naming: a future re-reading that moves one of these four rows must move it in both records, or the repository will hold two different answers to one licence question.

- **Cited for:** the construction of a validity range, which is the mechanism [the design record's](../measured-feedback-tuning-loop.md) shape-transfer section previously had to take from the [adaptive-query-processing survey's](#aqp-survey-fntdb-2007) description rather than from the paper. The paper defines it and states its direction of conservatism: a validity range for a plan edge is "an upper and lower bound on the number of rows flowing through e, such that if the range is violated at run-time, we can guarantee P is suboptimal with respect to the optimizer's cost model", and "This range is defined conservatively, i.e., even within the validity range P may become suboptimal with respect to alternative QEP we do consider. This conservative definition is fine, since we only want to avoid needless re-optimization." The construction is a by-product of pruning: "POP computes validity ranges during the plan enumeration and pruning phases of the optimizer through a plan sensitivity analysis. It iteratively narrows the validity range for each input to an operator of the currently optimal plans, when pruning alternative plans during optimization." Concretely, when the surviving plan prunes an alternative, "we determine if there exists an input cardinality c > e such that the cost functions cost(Palt, c) and cost(Popt, c) intersect. We do this by solving for the root of cost(Palt , c) – cost(Popt , c) = 0", by a modified Newton-Raphson iteration capped in practice at three steps, because analytic inversion is rejected: "it is not practical to analytically compute the validity ranges because the formulas will have to be continually updated as the code changes." The paper also states the construction's limits — "Our method is limited as it only detects suboptimality of the root operator and Popt and Palt must share the same input edges" — and its advantage over enumerating parametric plans: "we need not enumerate beforehand all possible optimal plans under all possible parameter values – we only need the cardinality ranges within which the chosen plan remains optimal."
- **What it settles, and what it leaves standing.** The design record's Rule 2 requires a Tiler validity region to be *declared* rather than derived and explicitly declines to claim that this matches POP. Having read the paper, that caution was **warranted**, and it now rests on a stated reason rather than on absence of evidence. Three properties of the POP construction do not transfer. First, a POP range is a claim about the *optimizer's own cost model* — the quoted definition says so — so it bounds where the model changes its mind, not where an observation of a machine remains valid; deriving a measured result's validity region from Tiler's analytic model would bound the wrong thing. Second, POP's conservatism runs in the direction that is safe *only because a recovery path exists*: the range is deliberately wider than the true optimality region, and a plan inside its range may already be suboptimal, which costs POP a missed re-optimization and nothing worse. Tiler AOT-compiles one artifact with no re-optimization, so a region wider than the truth means a stored result speaks for subjects it should not — the silently-wrong direction the design record forbids throughout. Third, the derivation consumes the pruned alternatives and their cost functions at the moment of pruning, and Tiler discards alternatives after selection. **No conclusion in the design record changes.** The paper is also the source of the record's earlier characterisation of the survey's account as incomplete, which it was: the survey states that ranges exist, and this paper states how one is computed and how conservative it is.
- **Also read, and not relied upon:** the paper's measured results (TPC-H robustness within a factor of two of optimal; a real-world DMV workload with "cardinality estimation errors ... exceed six orders of magnitude"; "incurring only a negligible overhead of around 2-3% of the overall query execution time for queries not benefiting from POP"; a worst observed regression of a factor of five). These are bounded to that paper's DBMS, schema, and hardware and no Tiler claim rests on them.
- **Said about another source in this record, and now checked against it:** the paper states, of [`graefe-ward-sigmod-1989`](#graefe-ward-sigmod-1989), that because optimizer cost functions "are also not smooth, not even always continuous", "simple binary-search techniques as in [GW89] will not work." More exactly, the query optimizer must determine the formulas to find the break-even point and to compare it with the actual selectivity, and include these formulas in the query evaluation plan." For joins the same idea is stated as a tree: the optimizer "must design an efficient decision procedure which can be executed when the query evaluation plan is activated. This decision procedure must have resolved the interdependence of partial decisions into a straight-forward decision tree, and include the break-even values between alternative plans." At run time, "When an access module is activated, the first step is to evaluate the decision tree. Concurrently with the evaluation of the decision tree, this step sets up the bindings between the components of the access module."
- **Measurement (this paper's, on a Sequent Symmetry with 16 MHz Intel 80386 CPUs, 3,600 KB of file-system buffer, and a 13,229-record utility-billing table).** Two hand-built plans were compared across output sizes: index-based B-tree scan with index nested-loops join, against file scans with hash join. Cost is "the sum of the CPU time in user mode and the estimated time spent performing I/O". At the extremes the paper states "If only one account satisfies the predicate, the index strategy is superior by a factor of 10. If all or almost all accounts qualify, it is inferior by a factor of more than 3." The tabulated totals bracketing those claims are 0.429 s against 4.563 s at one tuple printed, and 36.257 s against 10.713 s at 13,229. Bounded to that prototype and that host.

It did, and they do not fare equally — one is confirmed, the other is a misattribution in its mechanism, and a third claim carried alongside them is refuted in its premise. The three bullets below are those verdicts.
- **Cole and Graefe's "left two all-important questions unanswered" — confirmed, and if anything understated.** The paper's own §7 is titled *Current Work* and lists both questions as open. On engineering a compile-time optimizer: "We are currently designing and implementing modifications to the EXODUS Optimizer Generator [27, 28, 29] that enable generated optimizers to optimize queries with free variables by creating more than one query evaluation plan", and "The next step will to allow designation of free variables in queries." On which decisions to delay, the paper states the *property* it wants without a procedure for reaching it — "The techniques proposed here do not require that as many choices as possible must be delayed until run time. Their advantage is that they allow delaying exactly as many choices as advisable" — and explicitly defers the selection criterion to future work: "In order to keep this set small, plans with wide range of optimality must be selected. We call this a query evaluation plan's stability and will develop this concept is a later paper." **The strongest evidence is in the experiment itself.** The measured multi-plan module was built by hand, not produced by an optimizer — "We constructed a multi-plan access module containing the state records and support functions needed to execute this query using either B-tree scan and index nested loops join or file scans and hash join" — and the decision procedure was not exercised at all: "For the experiment, we 'forced' the choose-plan operator to use a plan of our choice, disreganding the threshold amount." So the 1989 paper measures the *value* of choosing dynamically while leaving both the choice of what to defer and the compile-time construction unimplemented, which is exactly Cole and Graefe's claim.
- **Cole and Graefe's minimal-decision-procedure attribution — the goal is correctly attributed, the mechanism is not.** The phrase "minimal decision procedure" appears nowhere in the 1989 paper, and neither does "inverse" or any cognate: `pdftotext -layout` over the digested copy matches `invers` zero times. The *goal* attribution is sound — a decision tree of break-even values precomputed at compile time, which is what the 1994 paper contrasts its own approach against. The *mechanism* attribution is not. The 1994 rejection reads "it is not easy to build these minimal decision procedures since it requires building inverses for all cost functions" and "In an extensible system, we consider it entirely unrealistic to assume that inverses of cost functions can be provided" — but the 1989 paper never asks for an analytic inverse. Its stated method is repeated *forward* evaluation: "the optimizer invokes the cost function repeatedly to determine the break-even point of the two plans, and optimizes the subranges separately." Numerical root-finding on a forward cost function is precisely not an inversion of it. **So the 1994 paper rejects a stronger requirement than its predecessor stated.** This does not rescue the 1989 design — see the refutation below — but it means the rejection as quoted is not a fair summary of what was proposed.
- **Markl et al.'s "simple binary-search techniques as in [GW89] will not work" — the citation is real, the premise about the paper is wrong.** The paper does propose a binary search: "The first experiment will involve a binary search scheme similar to the one proposed for XPRS [10]. The independent variable for the binary search can be the buffer size as in XPRS or the cardinality of an intermediate result, calculated from a variable binding." But it scopes it hard in the next sentence — "Clearly, only a very limited number of variables can be dealt with in this way, probably only one or two … For the more general problem of embedded queries with multiple free variables, the search space must be pruned more aggressively" — and, decisively, **it anticipates non-smooth cost functions explicitly and handles them outside the search rather than inside it**: "When a cost function is invoked, it first determines whether the cost increases 'smoothly' over the range of input sizes, or whether the cost function shows a significant discontinuity. If necessary, the range is split at points of discontinuity, and a choose-plan operator is introduced into the plan." Only then does iterative search run, and only within ranges already established as smooth: "If two methods with smooth cost functions show overlapping cost ranges … the optimizer invokes the cost function repeatedly to determine the break-even point." The paper even gives the canonical discontinuity as its worked example: "The cost function of hash join may indicate a discontinuity at the point where temporary files must be used to resolve hash table overflow. At this point, the range is split and two sub-plans are used." **Markl et al.'s objection identifies a real gap but misplaces it.** The gap is that the 1989 paper never says how the optimizer *determines* smoothness or *locates* the discontinuities — it asserts that the cost function "first determines" this and supplies no procedure. That is a genuine weakness and it is not the one the quoted sentence names.

- **The finding that would have mattered is refuted.** This record previously named one way the lookup could change a conclusion: if the 1989 minimal decision procedure were cheaper or more general than its successors describe, it would be a candidate for resolving a deferred cost row at a later availability phase *without carrying alternatives*, which is the one thing that would make the choose-plan line competitive with the [`AvailabilityPhase`](../../../decisions/0043-use-typed-phased-target-feasibility.md) ladder rather than a superset of it. Having read the paper: **cheaper, yes; more general, no; and it does not avoid carrying alternatives — it requires them.** The four bullets below carry that verdict and its consequence.
- **Cheaper at start-up, and by construction.** The 1989 decision is a comparison of run-time bindings against break-even values computed at compile time, so no cost function runs at start-up. The 1994 successor moved the cost evaluation *into* start-up — "The decision procedure is now merely a cost comparison of the plan alternatives with run-time bindings instantiated" — and paid 5.8 s of start-up CPU for its largest dynamic plan. On start-up cost alone the older design is the better one, which is the part of the hypothesis that survives.
- **But the alternatives are still shipped, stated by the paper in as many words.** "In addition to the decision tree designed by the optimizer, the access module must also contain the support functions for all possible query evaluation plans. These support functions include comparisons, hash functions, etc. The physical organization of the access module must allow equally efficient execution of any of the query evaluation plans." The retention obligation is therefore identical to 1994's, and the paper concedes it does not scale: "Since the number of possible join strategies is very large, even for only moderately complex queries, it is not possible to include all query execution plans in the access module." Its two proposed escapes are a heuristic subset — "the optimizer can select a subset of query execution plans … plans with wide range of optimality must be selected", the criterion deferred to a later paper — and component-level sharing, "instead of storing complete plans, only elements of the plans are stored, and linked together when the access module is activated", which is the DAG-sharing idea 1994 developed.
- **And it is strictly less general.** The 1989 paper carries **no optimality guarantee at all**. The guarantee that makes the line interesting — "Plans not included in a dynamic plan cannot possibly be optimal for any run-time binding of the unknown parameters" — is 1994's contribution, and it is exactly what the heuristic subset of 1989 gives up.
- **Consequence: no conclusion in the design record changes, and the choose-plan conclusion is strengthened a second time.** The design record's closing section named the choice of the `AvailabilityPhase` ladder over a run-time choose-plan operator as one of two conclusions thinner than the rest. Reading the 1994 paper made it *supported*; reading the 1989 origin makes it stronger still, because the origin carries the same artifact-size and retention obligation **without** the optimality guarantee that was the obligation's only justification. There is no earlier, cheaper form of choose-plan that resolves a deferred cost row without carrying alternatives. The candidate mechanism this record was looking for does not exist in the paper that was supposed to contain it.

- **Two observations recorded rather than acted on**, on the pattern [`cole-graefe-sigmod-1994`](#cole-graefe-sigmod-1994) uses for the same purpose. Neither is a conclusion and neither was acted on in the design record.
- **The break-even construction is POP's, fifteen years earlier, and the record should not have been surprised by [`markl-pop-sigmod-2004`](#markl-pop-sigmod-2004)'s.** Split ranges at discontinuities, then locate the crossover of two cost functions within a smooth subrange by repeated forward evaluation — that is structurally the same construction as POP "solving for the root of cost(Palt , c) – cost(Popt , c) = 0" by a capped Newton-Raphson iteration. Neither paper cites the other for it. This changes nothing in the design record, whose Rule 2 declines to derive validity regions from an analytic model for reasons that apply to both constructions equally; it is recorded because a reader tracing where derived validity regions come from should know the idea is older than the paper this record credits.
- **The paper states a measured-feedback ambition, and it is close enough to this design record's subject to flag.** §7.2 closes: "In a later stage, we intend to augment the selectivity estimation procedures and cost functions with observations from query evaluations. Thus, we will adjust and Iearn optimal values and formulas for these functions … Notice that only dynamic query evaluation plans allow incorporating cost function adjustments in existing access modules effectively, i.e., without recompilation, thus providing the most promising environment for learning and adjusting selectivity estimation and cost functions." The acknowledgements credit the idea: "Guy Lohman provided interesting and stimulating discussions, and pointed out the importance of learning cost functions from run time observations." **This does not contradict the design record's position that a choose-plan decision is not a measured-feedback mechanism**, and the two claims should not be conflated: the *decision* remains an analytic comparison in both papers, and what is claimed here is only that dynamic plans are a convenient host for cost-function coefficients that were learned elsewhere. It is also a stated intention in a 1989 prospectus, unimplemented and unmeasured, and its stated benefit — updating cost functions "without recompilation" — is worth nothing to a compiler whose model is ahead-of-time recompilation. **Fact, and no more:** the ambition is 1989, not a Tiler consideration.

- **Cited for:** what a run-time `choose-plan` operator actually is and what it costs. The deferral target is start-up time, not arbitrary run time: "The choose-plan operator allows postponement of the choice among two or more equivalent, alternative plans until start-up-time, when the decision can be based on up-to-date knowledge, e.g., the bindings of user variables unbound at compile-time." The enabling concept is compile-time cost *incomparability*: "we acknowledge that missing run-time bindings may render it impossible to calculate and compare the costs of alternative plans at compile-time. The decision procedure is deliberately not an inverted cost function — "it is not easy to build these minimal decision procedures since it requires building inverses for all cost functions", and "In an extensible system, we consider it entirely unrealistic to assume that inverses of cost functions can be provided" — but a re-evaluation of the same cost functions with bindings supplied: "The decision procedure is now merely a cost comparison of the plan alternatives with run-time bindings instantiated; thus, the reasons for incomparability of costs at compile-time have vanished." The guarantee it buys is stated as "Plans not included in a dynamic plan cannot possibly be optimal for any run-time binding of the unknown parameters, and a dynamic plan is guaranteed to include all potentially optimal plans for all run-time bindings", and it requires a shared representation: "all plans and alternative plans must be represented as directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) with common subexpressions, not as trees."
